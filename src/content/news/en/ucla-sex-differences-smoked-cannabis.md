For years it has been said that women are more sensitive to the effects of cannabis. A study from the UCLA Center for Cannabis and Cannabinoids, published this month in *Neuropsychopharmacology*, suggests the answer depends on something else as well: **how often each person uses**.

## What was done

- **Study type:** a laboratory experiment with healthy adults, **placebo-controlled** and blinded: in separate sessions each participant smoked cannabis with no THC, lower THC and higher THC, without knowing which one they had.
- **Who:** the group was split evenly by sex and by frequency of use (weekly or daily).
- **Hormones controlled:** women were not using hormonal contraception and were tested in the same phase of the menstrual cycle.
- **Funding:** public, from the US National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA).

## What it found

- **Men who use occasionally** reported feeling more high, more sedated and more impaired, with more unpleasant effects such as anxiety. They were the only group whose heart rate rose clearly.
- Women and frequent users of both sexes responded in similar ways.
- The difference is **not explained by blood THC**: men who use daily had the highest levels and still felt fewer effects than occasional users.
- Regardless of sex, frequent users showed more signs of dependence.

## What this study does not say

The authors name three limits themselves: the sample was **modest**, several results are **exploratory**, and the cannabis used had **less THC than many products sold today**. Only smoked cannabis and short-term effects were studied. The team is now preparing studies with stronger products and with adults over 55.

> One small study does not change what we know, but it is a useful reminder: two people with the same blood THC can have very different experiences.

## Why it matters to us

Many roadside tests and much "dosing" advice assume that the same THC means the same effect. This work suggests that use history and sex both matter, and that more research is needed before generalising.

This story is education, not medical advice. Source: UCLA Health press release of 5 November 2026 and the paper in *Neuropsychopharmacology* ([doi:10.1038/s41386-026-02571-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41386-026-02571-3)).
