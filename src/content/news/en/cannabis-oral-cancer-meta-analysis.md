Tobacco, alcohol and the human papillomavirus (HPV) are known causes of oral cancer. With legalisation spreading, the obvious question is: what about cannabis? A systematic review and meta-analysis published on 10 November in *Health Science Reports* pulls together what has been published so far.

## What was done

- **Study type:** systematic review and meta-analysis. It is not a new experiment: it combines and re-analyses earlier studies.
- **Search:** PubMed, EMBASE and Web of Science up to August 2025. Out of 1,063 records, **11 studies** met the criteria, with **245,594 participants** in total.
- **Which studies:** all observational (case-control and cohort). None is a randomised controlled trial.
- **Who wrote it:** a team from universities in Pakistan, India and Uganda.

## What it found

- Pooling everything, people who used cannabis were **less likely to have oral cancer** than those who did not (OR 0.48; 95% CI 0.31–0.76).
- That result comes mainly from **case-control** studies (OR 0.46).
- In **cohort** studies, which follow people over time and are usually more reliable for measuring risk, there was **no clear association** either way (OR 2.51; 95% CI 0.24–25.8, a huge range).
- Results varied **a lot between studies** (high heterogeneity).

## What this study does not say

**It does not say that cannabis protects against cancer.** The authors themselves warn that the inverse association only shows up in one type of study, is not confirmed by cohorts and may be due to factors that were not measured well (residual confounding). Their conclusion is more modest: **current evidence does not support cannabis use as an independent risk factor for oral cancer**, and prospective studies are needed.

Some outlets have run headlines saying cannabis "may lower the risk". That is exactly the kind of headline worth reading closely.

> Not finding a higher risk is not the same as proving that something is safe or protective.

## Why it matters to us

Smoking cannabis means inhaling smoke and, often, mixing it with tobacco, which is a known cause of oral cancer. This paper does not change that. What it adds is clarity: it separates what we know (no clear sign of a risk specific to cannabis) from what we do not yet know (whether there is any effect, and in which direction).
